A Mining CEO Blamed AI. The Blockchain Kept Its Own Notes.

Luxor's chief executive called it an AI-enabled attack and apologized for it. Then the pool paid out twice to a single address, and the document that would settle the question did not appear.

Style: Andy GreenbergStatus: reviewReport 466

The address on X

Fourteen hours before Hansen's post, at 8:08 p.m. Pacific on Saturday, a mining hosting company called CoreX Hosting had already gone public with a warning about its own farm.

"Just a heads up, there is a big miner hack going on as we speak," the company wrote. "Miners are being pointed at @ocean_mining with BTC address bc1qphukp9rjfc4rcej7erpm9ae20xmtspw8pcddrr." CoreX said its entire farm had been hijacked, named Luxor Commander as the reason, and added that Luxor had "desperately tried to turn off all the commander support to control the situation." (CoreX Hosting)

The address was the detail that would outlast the weekend. Forty-two characters, posted by a hosting company at eight in the evening, and the one detail in the whole story that would end up with a public record.

At 3:57 UTC on Sunday, CoreX reported the attack was still running. The address was still drawing around 13 exahashes per second, a measure of mining power; one exahash is a quintillion hash attempts a second, and CoreX was fixing machines by hand. "I am changing miners back as I can," it wrote. (Altcoin Buzz)

By then, two accounts of the night were running on different clocks.

One command

It helps to know what Commander is, because the breach makes little sense without it.

Commander is a dashboard for mining operations: real-time monitoring, remote commands, automated profitability tuning. One of those remote commands is called Change Pool Settings, and it edits the three things that decide where a miner's work is credited, the pool's stratum URL, the worker name, and the password. Luxor's own documentation describes pushing that change across hundreds or thousands of machines in a single bulk action. Other commands cover firmware installs, reboots, sleep and wake, and power adjustments. Every one of them is logged in an Actions Log that records the action type, who or what created it, the site, the agent, the miners affected, and the time. (Luxor Commander docs)

That is the product's value, and it was the attack surface. No private key had to be stolen. No rig had to be touched. No firmware had to be rewritten. An attacker who reached the dashboard could move the work somewhere else by editing a field, and the machines would comply because complying is their job.

TFTC put it in one line: "The convenience of centralized fleet management and the risk of centralized fleet management are the same feature." OCEAN, the pool the hashrate was sent to, pays miners directly from the coinbase transaction and never holds their funds, which blunts the theft version of this attack. It does nothing about the redirection version, because the redirection happened a layer above the pool. (TFTC)

Two ledgers

The scale is still being argued about, and the argument is instructive.

CoreX's early estimate was roughly 64,000 machines and about 13 EH/s, which would have been around 1.4% of a network hashing at roughly 934 EH/s. Luxor has confirmed neither number. The company has said only "just over a dozen client accounts." CoreX later reported the unauthorized hashrate had fallen to about 100 petahashes per second. TFTC noted that OCEAN's public dashboard briefly spiked to around 50 EH/s during the incident, dashboard data, not an independently verified measurement. (MLQ.ai; TFTC)

The money is smaller and better documented, and it came in two pieces.

On the evening of Nov 15, mempool.space developer Mononaut looked at OCEAN's dashboard and saw about 0.657 BTC of estimated rewards, which he described as "shares earned from stolen hash." Estimated, not paid: at that point the blockchain showed no confirmed incoming transactions to the address. (Bitcoin.com News)

Then the blocks arrived. Altcoin Buzz traced two coinbase outputs to the address CoreX had posted: block 975,995 at 17:22:50 UTC paid 0.08088977 BTC, and block 976,029 at 22:54:00 UTC paid 0.08113106 BTC. Total, 0.16202083 BTC, about $10,512 at the $64,883 price CoinGecko recorded on Nov 16. The attackers solo-mined nothing; under OCEAN's proportional, TIDES-style distribution, the payouts simply reflected work that had already been done. The outputs were unspent as of Nov 16. (Altcoin Buzz; Coinpedia)

Two numbers, 0.657 and 0.162, and no public source that reconciles them beyond the obvious: estimated dashboard rewards are not final coinbase payments.

The timestamps are less comfortable. Luxor says Commander was patched within two hours of detection. The payouts landed at 17:22 and 22:54 UTC on Nov 15, and CoreX had described the redirect as still live at 03:57 UTC. A pool can pay for work performed earlier, so the later times do not by themselves prove the redirect survived the patch. But nobody has published the accounting that would show it didn't.

The missing document

Here is what Luxor has not published: the post-mortem.

There is no advisory, no CVE, no commit, no excerpt from the Actions Log naming the account or the automation rule that pushed the pool change. Commander is proprietary software, so the fix was server-side and there is nothing for a customer to install. As of Nov 17, the promised write-up had not appeared in any coverage reviewed for this piece. (Altcoin Buzz; TFTC)

Which leaves the AI claim where it started: with its author. The phrase is Hansen's, no Luxor filing or advisory has elaborated on it, and the detail he has offered is atmospheric rather than technical, that "abliterated models," open-weight models with their safety training stripped out, are "running 24/7 against your entire surface, looking for a little crack, which they then use to (almost instantly) expand." Luxor has put most new feature work on hold and bought bare-metal GPUs to run those same models defensively, while rotating keys, updating kernels, and pinning dependencies. (Decrypt; TFTC)

CoreX, for its part, suggested the problem might have been closer to the ground: it said it had "isolated what I think is the program used," pointing at something on the operator side. Whether the entry point was Luxor's own infrastructure or a customer's Commander account is unresolved in public.

There is an awkward symmetry here. Luxor announced Luxor AI in September 2026, extending its mining infrastructure into AI and high-performance computing, and raised $16 million last month in a round led by CMT Digital, with Canaan, MicroBT, Halo, and Steelhead participating. The company now warning the industry about AI-enabled attackers is also selling AI to the industry. (The Block)

None of that makes Hansen wrong. AI-assisted intrusion is a documented pattern, as TFTC notes: Anthropic's September 2026 threat-intelligence report described "automated exploit foundries," and Chainalysis disclosed on Oct 30 that an attacker using "offensive agentic techniques" reached customer identity data. The question is narrower. A confession is not an incident report, and the sentence "you trust us with your fleet, and we need to be better" is only checkable against the document Luxor has promised.

At 10:17 on Sunday, that sentence read as accountability. Two days later, the only thing standing behind it is a post, an address on X, and 0.162 BTC that, as of Nov 16, had not moved.
