Luxor Calls It an AI Attack. The Post-Mortem Has to Prove It.

Luxor says a "sophisticated AI-enabled vector" let someone retarget just over a dozen customer mining fleets to another pool. The company's timeline, the onchain record and an unpublished post-mortem leave the central claim untested.

Luxor Calls It an AI Attack. The Post-Mortem Has to Prove It.

Luxor says a "sophisticated AI-enabled vector" let someone retarget just over a dozen customer mining fleets to another pool. The company's timeline, the onchain record and an unpublished post-mortem leave the central claim untested.

The scene

The address showed up in the miners' settings first: bc1qphukp9rjfc4rcej7erpm9ae20xmtspw8pcddrr, a string with no name attached, sitting in the field where a machine is told which pool to work for and which address to pay.

At 3:08 UTC on Sunday, 8:08 on Saturday night on the U.S. West Coast, an operator called CoreX Hosting posted it to X. "Just a heads up, there is a big miner hack going on as we speak," the post read. "Miners are being pointed at @ocean_mining with BTC address bc1qphukp9rjfc4rcej7erpm9ae20xmtspw8pcddrr."

CoreX said its entire farm had been hijacked, and it named the instrument: Luxor Commander.

Commander is Luxor's fleet-management software. One dashboard holds a live view of every rig, and bulk remote commands can reach hundreds or thousands of machines at once. Among them is "Change Pool Settings," which rewrites the pool address, worker name and password on selected miners. The same menu reboots machines, installs and uninstalls firmware, and puts them to sleep. Each action is supposed to land in an Actions Log with the creator, the site, the miners affected, the timestamp and the status, according to Luxor's own documentation.

That is the product. It is also the attack surface.

The discovery

At 17:17 UTC on Sunday, Nick Hansen, Luxor's chief executive, posted on X. "The last 12 hours have been pretty rough," he wrote. Around 6:00 p.m. Pacific time the previous day, "a subset of Commander customers were compromised, through a sophisticated AI-enabled vector. We were able to identify and patch quickly so that it didn't continue, then the hard work of reverting started."

He did not say how the attack worked. Decrypt's account notes that gap plainly.

Eight minutes later, Luxor's corporate account filled in the operational picture. The statement said internal monitoring had flagged an unauthorized redirect of hashrate through Commander, hashrate being the computing power miners point at the network to earn rewards. Just over a dozen client accounts were affected, with their machines pointed to a different pool for a limited window. Commander was patched within two hours and restoration began. No data was leaked or exfiltrated. The problem was isolated to Commander; Luxor Pool and the LuxOS firmware were unaffected. Every affected client would be paid in full for the redirected hashrate, "as if it had mined normally." A post-mortem was coming.

Hansen added an apology: "You trust us with your fleet, and we need to be better."

Then CoreX posted again.

At 03:57 UTC, roughly three hours after the start time Hansen had given, the operator said the attack was still running. The address was still at about 13 exahashes per second (EH/s). "I am changing miners back as I can," CoreX wrote, according to Altcoin Buzz.

Two accounts of the same night, and they did not fit together.

The escalation

The scale CoreX described was industrial. Roughly 64,000 machines, the operator estimated, representing about 13 EH/s, approximately 1.4% of Bitcoin's network hashrate at the time, which it put at roughly 934 EH/s. Luxor has confirmed neither the machine count nor the total. TFTC reported that OCEAN's public dashboard briefly spiked to around 50 EH/s during the incident, though that figure comes from dashboard data rather than independent verification. CoreX later said the unauthorized hashrate had fallen to about 100 petahashes per second (PH/s).

The money was smaller than the machine count suggested, and harder to pin down.

On Sunday, mempool.space developer Mononaut looked at OCEAN's dashboard and gave Bitcoin.com News a working estimate: "Oof, about 0.657 BTC of shares earned from stolen hash, per ocean's dashboard." Estimated shares are not completed payments. At the time, blockchain records showed no confirmed incoming transactions to the address.

By the next day, Altcoin Buzz's onchain review had found two coinbase outputs to it: block 975,995 at 17:22:50 UTC on Nov 15 paid 0.08088977 BTC, and block 976,029 at 22:54:00 UTC paid 0.08113106 BTC. The balance was 0.16202083 BTC, about $10,512 at the $64,883 price CoinGecko recorded on Nov 16. Per TFTC, the outputs were unspent as of Nov 16, and the attackers never solo-mined a block; the payments reflect OCEAN's proportional TIDES-style distribution to redirected hashrate.

The 0.657 BTC estimate and the 0.162 BTC that actually landed have never been reconciled in public. Nor have the payment times. If the redirect began at 6:00 p.m. PDT and Commander was patched within two hours, the window closed around 03:00 UTC. The first payout arrived roughly fourteen hours after that. Proportional pool payouts can lag the work that earned them, so the timestamps do not by themselves prove the redirect continued. They are still sitting there, unexplained.

The turn

Read the phrase again: "a sophisticated AI-enabled vector."

It describes how someone got in. It says nothing about what they did once inside, and Luxor has published nothing that shows a model wrote the exploit, found the hole, or drove the intrusion. Multiple outlets, including TFTC and Altcoin Buzz, treat the AI attribution as Hansen's self-reported framing rather than an independently verified description of the attack. Altcoin Buzz's headline asks the question directly: was AI behind it?

Hansen's follow-up post widened the claim rather than narrowing it. "Abliterated models are running 24/7 against your entire surface, looking for a little crack, which they then use to (almost instantly) expand." Abliterated models are open-weight AI systems with their safety guardrails stripped, the kind that will write exploit code without refusing. Crypto businesses, he said, are "inundated with AI-enabled attacks."

CoreX pointed somewhere else. The operator said it had "isolated what I think is the program used," suggesting something running on the operator side rather than a confirmed breach of Luxor's platform. Whether the compromise was vendor-side or client-side remains unresolved in public.

There is also an awkward fact for the framing. Luxor announced "Luxor AI" in September 2026, extending its mining infrastructure into AI and high-performance computing, and raised $16 million last month in a round led by CMT Digital, with Canaan, MicroBT, Halo and Steelhead participating, The Block reported. The vendor blaming AI for the breach also sells AI to the same industry.

None of that makes the AI story false. The threat is real enough elsewhere: TFTC notes that Anthropic's September 2026 threat-intelligence report described "automated exploit foundries," and that Chainalysis disclosed on Oct 30, 2026 that an attacker using "offensive agentic techniques" reached customer identity data.

But here is what the headline hides. Whatever got the attacker through the door, the redirect itself needed no model at all. A pool change is a feature. It required no miner keys, no malicious firmware, no change to Bitcoin's protocol, only access to the dashboard that already had the authority to move thousands of machines.

If a model was involved, it was the lockpick. The theft was the product.

What it means

The direct loss is small: about $10.5K confirmed, against a fleet-management platform used across the industry. The exposure is not.

"The convenience of centralized fleet management and the risk of centralized fleet management are the same feature," TFTC wrote. One compromised credential, API endpoint or session token inherits the operator's ability to reconfigure a fleet. OCEAN's noncustodial design, direct coinbase payouts, no pool-held funds, limits the theft version of the attack. It does nothing about redirection, because the redirection happens above the pool, in the software that tells machines where to mine.

Bitcoin's consensus layer was never touched. No blocks were manipulated, no transactions double-spent. At most, on CoreX's unverified estimate, about 1.4% of network hashrate moved for a while.

The practical lesson is unglamorous. TFTC's recommendation is to treat fleet-management access like a hardware wallet: multi-factor authentication, hardware keys, audit logs, an emergency revocation path. Commander has an audit trail. No public report has shared what it recorded, who created the pool-change actions, from which site or agent, at what time.

And the document that would settle the entry point, the AI involvement and the timeline had not been published as of Nov 17. There is no CVE and no advisory, because Commander is proprietary software and the fix was server-side at Luxor.

Which leaves the address. It has not moved: two payouts, 0.16202083 BTC, unspent as of Nov 16, sitting in a string that first appeared in a miner's settings on a Saturday night.

The post-mortem will say whether a machine picked that lock or a person with a password did. For the operators still running one dashboard over thousands of rigs, the difference matters less than the fact that the door was there.
