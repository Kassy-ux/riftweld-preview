Bulk Discount: How a Fleet Manager's Pool Setting Redirected Bitcoin Hashrate

Luxor patched a fleet-management compromise within two hours of detecting it, promised to pay clients in full, and called the cause an AI-enabled vector. The redirect is documented. The AI is not.

Style: James KettleStatus: review

Outline

I'll start with what Luxor confirmed, then the Commander feature that makes a redirect cheap, then the two loss figures that refuse to reconcile, then the AI claim and what would settle it. The short version: a dozen accounts, a pool change, and a post-mortem nobody outside Luxor has read.

Background

The assumption is that stealing hashrate means touching miners, a firmware implant, a lifted key, something physical on the rig. It stops holding when the fleet manager ships a bulk command called Change Pool Settings that reaches thousands of machines in one workflow.

On Saturday, Nov 14, 2026, around 6:00 p.m. Pacific, someone used that reach. Luxor's internal monitoring flagged an unauthorized redirect of hashrate through Commander, its fleet-management software. The on-call engineering team patched Commander within two hours of detection and began restoring affected machines to their original pool settings. Luxor's own statement says the impact was "just over a dozen client accounts," that no data was leaked or exfiltrated, and that the issue was isolated to Commander, Luxor Pool and LuxOS were untouched. Every affected client will be paid in full for the redirected hashrate, "as if it had mined normally." A post-mortem is promised.

The first alarm did not come from Luxor. CoreX Hosting posted at 03:08 UTC on Nov 15: "Just a heads up, there is a big miner hack going on as we speak. Miners are being pointed at @ocean_mining with BTC address bc1qphukp9rjfc4rcej7erpm9ae20xmtspw8pcddrr." CoreX said its entire farm was hijacked and named Commander. Luxor's public confirmation landed at 17:25 UTC, by which point the address had a name, a pool, and an audience.

How the redirect works

Commander is Luxor's SaaS for running a mining fleet: monitoring, bulk commands, automated profitability tuning. The command that matters here is Change Pool Settings, which updates the stratum URL, worker name and password for selected miners, and per Luxor's documentation, those selections can run across hundreds or thousands of machines in a single bulk-action workflow. Every action lands in an Actions Log that records action type, creator (User, Automation Rule/Trigger, or Intelligent Miner), site, agent, miners affected, timestamp and status.

Nobody has shown us that audit trail.

The structural point is simpler than the tooling. Redirecting work does not require touching a miner or stealing a key. The rig dutifully hashes; it has simply been told to send the result somewhere else. TFTC put it well: "the convenience of centralized fleet management and the risk of centralized fleet management are the same feature." OCEAN's noncustodial design, direct coinbase payouts, the pool never holding miner funds, limits the theft variant. It does nothing about the redirection variant, because the redirect happens one layer up.

The attack surface was a settings field.

The two takings

Two numbers circulate. They are not the same number, and the gap is instructive.

The first is an estimate. Bitcoin.com News, citing mempool.space developer Mononaut, reported about 0.657 BTC of "shares earned from stolen hash" on OCEAN's dashboard. Estimated rewards are not payments; at that point no confirmed transactions had reached the address.

The second is settled. Altcoin Buzz's onchain review found exactly two transactions, both coinbase outputs: block 975,995 at 17:22:50 UTC on Nov 15 paid 0.08088977 BTC, and block 976,029 at 22:54:00 UTC paid 0.08113106 BTC. Balance: 0.16202083 BTC, about $10,512 at the $64,883 price CoinGecko recorded on Nov 16. TFTC notes the outputs were unspent as of Nov 16 and that the attackers solo-mined nothing; under OCEAN's TIDES distribution, an address is paid a proportional share of blocks found by others in the reward window.

So the discrepancy is probably dashboard estimate versus finalised coinbase payment, with the usual lag. No public source reconciles them, which matters because the same lag weakens later payout timestamps as evidence that the redirect persisted.

Scale is murkier. CoreX estimated roughly 64,000 machines and about 13 EH/s, around 1.4% of a network hashrate of roughly 934 EH/s at the time, then reported the address down to about 100 PH/s. TFTC saw OCEAN's dashboard briefly spike to about 50 EH/s. Luxor has confirmed neither the machine count nor the hashrate. A dozen accounts and 64,000 machines can both be true if an account fronts a large farm, or they can both be numbers nobody has audited. Luxor's statement counts accounts, not rigs.

Luxor says Commander was patched within two hours of detection. CoreX was still describing the attack as ongoing at 03:57 UTC on Nov 15, roughly three hours after the reported 6:00 p.m. PDT start, with the address at about 13 EH/s: "I am changing miners back as I can." TIDES lag may explain the later payouts. It does not, on its own, close the gap between "patched" and "still hashing at the address", the detection time has not been published. Luxor's promised post-mortem is the obvious place to reconcile them.

The AI question

Hansen's phrase is "a sophisticated AI-enabled vector." He did not detail how the attack worked. In a follow-up he described crypto businesses as "inundated with AI-enabled attacks," with abliterated models, open-weight models with the safety guardrails stripped, probing systems around the clock. Luxor's response was to buy bare-metal GPUs to run such models defensively, pause most new feature work, rotate keys, update kernels and pin dependencies.

I went looking for the technical record and there isn't one. No post-mortem, no CVE, no advisory, no commit. Commander is proprietary SaaS and the fix was applied on Luxor's side, so there is no patch for a customer to diff. The claim is currently a sentence from a CEO, and the sharpest scepticism comes from the reporting: Altcoin Buzz's headline asks whether AI was behind the attack at all, and notes that CoreX said it had "isolated what I think is the program used", pointing at something on the operator side rather than a confirmed breach of Luxor's platform. Whether this was vendor-side or client-side remains unresolved in public.

The AI story is not implausible. TFTC points at Anthropic's September 2026 threat-intelligence report on "automated exploit foundries" and Chainalysis's Oct 30, 2026 disclosure that an attacker using "offensive agentic techniques" reached customer identity data. Continuous, cheap probing is real. But "AI was involved somewhere" and "AI enabled this specific redirect" are different claims, and only one of them is currently supported. The Block adds the uncomfortable detail that Luxor announced Luxor AI in September 2026 and raised $16 million last month, led by CMT Digital, with Canaan, MicroBT, Halo and Steelhead participating. A vendor can sell AI and be attacked by AI. It can also prefer that framing to a more mundane account compromise.

Defence

What should an operator check? The controls are unglamorous and they all sit on the management plane.

  • Inventory who can issue bulk pool changes, and require hardware keys or at minimum MFA for those accounts. A fleet manager is wallet-adjacent infrastructure; treat it like one.
  • Alert on any change to stratum URL, worker name or pool password, at the machine level, not only in the vendor's UI. The Actions Log is useful after the fact; a local diff wakes you up.
  • Read the Actions Log for creator and agent fields after any unexplained payout shift. The schema separates a human user from an Automation Rule and an Intelligent Miner, exactly the triage you want.
  • Keep an emergency revocation path that does not depend on the vendor's status page. CoreX's account suggests operators were changing miners back by hand while the platform was being patched.
  • Watch the payout address, not just the hashrate graph. CoreX saw the redirect in its own telemetry before any formal disclosure did.

The vendor-side tell is in Luxor's own response: feature work paused, keys rotated, kernels updated, dependencies pinned. If a fleet-management vendor has not done that arithmetic, ask why.

Takeaways

The redirect is the confirmed part. A bulk settings command, a pool change, a dozen accounts, two coinbase payouts totalling 0.16202083 BTC, a two-hour patch, and a compensation promise. The protocol did not blink; no keys were needed; no miner was touched.

The unconfirmed part is larger than the confirmed part. The entry point, the AI involvement, the true scale and the true take all wait on a post-mortem that had not appeared by Nov 17. Until it does, the honest summary is a settings field with too much reach and a cause that has been named but not shown.

The tooling, such as it is: the Actions Log, a pool dashboard, and a block explorer. No CVE to track, no patch to apply. Dry enough.
